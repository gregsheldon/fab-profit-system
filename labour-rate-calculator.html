THE EXPENSIVE GUESS

A WAGE IS NOT A LABOUR RATE.

Paying a fabricator $25 an hour does not mean the labour costs your company $25 an hour. Paid holidays, vacation, cleanup, loading, meetings, mistakes, rent, trucks, office wages, insurance, software, and every non-billable hour still have to be recovered.

If those costs are not inside your rate, they come out of your profit.

WHAT IT CALCULATES

ONE RATE. EVERY REAL COST BEHIND IT.

Enter the blue cells. The green cells do the math. The yellow result gives you the recommended billable labour rate.

01

Wages + payroll burden

Turn hourly pay into the real direct cost after employer taxes, workers’ comp, vacation burden, and benefits.

02

Paid lost time

Account for stat holidays, sick days, and paid vacation before assuming all 2,080 hours are productive.

03

Applied time

Adjust for cleanup, loading, meetings, travel, mistakes, and normal shop downtime.

04

Overhead recovery

Spread rent, trucks, admin, insurance, utilities, software, and fixed costs across real billable hours.

05

True break-even

See the hourly rate where the company covers its costs but makes zero profit.

06

Profit + tax target

Work backward from the profit you want to keep and calculate the rate required to get there.

A REALITY CHECK

$25 PER HOUR CAN REQUIRE A $136.94 RATE.

In the calculator’s worked example, a $25 top wage becomes $42.02 per billable hour after payroll burden, paid lost time, and applied time. After overhead, break-even rises to $104.72. Add the selected profit and tax targets, and the recommended rate becomes $136.94.
